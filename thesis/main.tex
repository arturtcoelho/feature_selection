\textbf{Abstract}
\end{center}

Feature selection is a fundamental preprocessing step in machine learning that can improve model generalization, reduce computational cost, and enhance interpretability. While SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) has gained widespread adoption for model explanation, its utility as a feature selection criterion remains underexplored in systematic comparisons.

This thesis evaluates SHAP-based feature selection for tabular regression, comparing it against established alternatives including mutual information, recursive feature elimination, and native tree-based importance. The evaluation follows a two-stage protocol: a broad screening benchmark across four datasets (Allstate Claims Severity, Bike Sharing, Communities and Crime, and Superconductor) and three model families (Ridge, ExtraTrees, and XGBoost), followed by a focused deep dive using XGBoost on two selected datasets with custom SHAP recursive elimination and hybrid strategies.

Results indicate that SHAP-based selection can achieve competitive predictive performance, occasionally obtaining the best RMSE at intermediate feature retention levels. However, these gains are neither systematic nor substantial. In the broad benchmark, SHAP wins in only 2 of 12 dataset-model configurations, both with XGBoost. In the focused evaluation, SHAP-based methods produce marginal improvements (typically less than 1\% relative) while hybrid strategies incur 20-30 times higher computational cost with no accuracy benefit.

The central conclusion is that SHAP represents a useful but expensive option for feature selection: a slight performance edge in select contexts, at the cost of increased runtime and---in some settings---reduced selection stability. For practitioners working under computational constraints, simpler methods such as mutual information or native importance remain the pragmatic default. SHAP's primary value continues to lie in its explanatory power rather than as a feature selection criterion.
